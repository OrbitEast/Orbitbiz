GUIDE · INVENTORY

What is inventory management?

Inventory management is the process of knowing what stock a business has, where it is, how it changes and when more stock needs to be purchased. Good inventory records connect operational activity with purchasing and sales.

Inventory is more than a stock number

A useful inventory record can include an item, quantity, unit, warehouse or location, purchase history, stock movements and adjustments. The goal is to explain why the current quantity exists, not simply display a number.

What is a stock movement?

A stock movement records a change in inventory. Examples include receiving purchased goods, selling goods, transferring stock between locations, returning items or making an approved adjustment. Movement history creates an audit trail around the current balance.

The purchase-to-stock connection

When a business orders products from a supplier, the purchase order describes what is expected. Receiving the goods confirms what actually arrived. The inventory record can then be updated from the receiving event rather than from an unrelated manual change.

The sales-to-stock connection

When an item is sold, the sale can affect available inventory. Keeping sales and inventory records connected reduces the chance that staff maintain two conflicting versions of stock.

Why inventory accuracy matters

  • Sales staff can see what is available.
  • Purchasing decisions can use current stock information.
  • Owners can investigate unusual adjustments.
  • Reports can connect operational activity with stock changes.
  • Customers are less likely to be promised unavailable items.

A simple inventory workflow

  1. Create clear item records.
  2. Record opening or starting quantities.
  3. Receive purchased stock through the purchasing workflow.
  4. Reduce or otherwise update stock through sales and approved movements.
  5. Review adjustments and discrepancies.
  6. Use reports to identify items that need attention.

Inventory vs stock

Stock usually refers to the goods currently held by the business. Inventory management is broader: it covers the records, processes and decisions used to control those goods.
